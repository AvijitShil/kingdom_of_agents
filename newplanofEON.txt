EON 2.0 — Evolutionary Civilizational Framework
PROPOSED RESEARCH AND ARCHITECTURE SPECIFICATION · VERSION 2.0

Before the full draft, there are three design decisions I want to make explicit.

1. Tokens are survival currency, not merely money. Every agent needs tokens to pay for its simulated existence. Agents can earn, trade, borrow, save, lose, steal or accumulate them through whatever activities the simulation permits. Wealth therefore becomes a matter of survival, influence and social power.

2. Reproduction is an agent decision, not a researcher-imposed family-size rule. There is no fixed family-size limit. The baseline simulation will support a maximum creation throughput of 10 new agents per simulated day across the civilization. Parents decide whether to create them and must consider their future upkeep. A separate experiment can remove the throughput limit.

3. Survival instincts operate exclusively inside EON. Agents can defend their simulated lives, families, wealth and kingdoms. They cannot acquire real-world resources, escape the simulation, create uncontrolled external processes or resist the researcher's termination controls.

There is also an important scientific distinction: because your foundation model remains frozen, this is primarily cultural, behavioural and institutional evolution, rather than genetic evolution of the neural model itself. The new generation can acquire different memories, skills, beliefs and strategies without changing the underlying model weights.
EON 2.0: A Framework for Cultural Evolution, Survival Economics, and Emergent Artificial Civilizations
Project: EON — Evolving Open-ended Network

1. Research Motivation
EON aims to investigate whether autonomous language-model agents can develop increasingly complex societies through survival pressure, social interaction, economic competition, reproduction, knowledge transmission, and institutional formation.

Unlike conventional multi-agent simulations that explicitly prescribe agent personalities, political ideologies, social roles, and expected behaviours, EON aims to provide a relatively open environment in which these properties can emerge through interactions between agents.

Agents are not instructed to create casinos, criminal organizations, political parties, religious groups, monopolies, or kingdoms. Instead, they receive the ability to communicate, trade, form relationships, create businesses, reproduce, establish institutions, negotiate agreements, and compete for limited resources.

The research investigates whether these underlying capabilities produce recognizable social structures and behavioural patterns over multiple generations.

The central principle is:

Do not prescribe what civilization should become. Establish the conditions under which civilization can develop.
1.1 Primary Research Question
Can a population of autonomous agents using an immutable foundation model develop cumulative cultural evolution, intergenerational behavioural transmission, economic institutions, cooperative structures, antisocial organizations, and independent political systems through interactions under survival-driven resource constraints?
1.2 Secondary Research Questions
1. How do agents develop habits, preferences, beliefs, and social behaviours?
2. How are behaviours transmitted between parents, children, peers, and influential agents?
3. Can antisocial behaviours emerge without explicitly programming them?
4. How do gambling businesses, monopolies, corruption, underground markets, and exploitative institutions emerge?
5. Does competition for survival produce cooperation, selfishness, or both?
6. How do families evolve into larger clans and political kingdoms?
7. How do agents develop different cultural identities?
8. How does wealth concentration affect reproduction, education, and political influence?
9. Can a civilization independently develop systems of law, taxation, education, trade, and military organization?
10. Do new generations preserve, reject, or transform the practices of their predecessors?
2. Foundational Evolutionary Theory
EON should incorporate cultural evolutionary theory rather than rely exclusively on predefined ideological doctrines.

Cultural evolution describes the development of socially transmitted information, including behaviours, beliefs, knowledge, skills, technologies, and practices.

EON adapts this idea into an artificial social environment.

The proposed evolutionary framework contains six mechanisms.
2.1 Variation: Creation of New Behaviours
Agents must be capable of developing new behaviours, practices, businesses, beliefs, strategies, and institutions.

An agent should not be restricted to the behaviours explicitly listed in its initial knowledge base.

Examples include:
- Developing a new form of business.
- Creating a wagering activity.
- Introducing a new method of trading.
- Developing a family tradition.
- Establishing a new social gathering.
- Introducing a new political arrangement.
- Creating an organization to protect economic interests.
- Developing a new belief concerning wealth, loyalty, or survival.
New practices should emerge from individual experimentation and interactions.

Every newly observed cultural practice should receive an identifiable record containing its creator, origin, description, adoption history, and subsequent transformations.
2.2 Transmission: Learning From Others
Agents should acquire knowledge through multiple transmission pathways.

Vertical transmission: Parents educate their children and transfer family traditions.

Horizontal transmission: Agents learn from friends, business partners, competitors, and peers.

Oblique transmission: Younger agents learn from older agents, experienced individuals, teachers, and influential community members.

Prestige-based transmission: Agents may imitate individuals who possess high status or influence.

Success-based transmission: Agents may imitate practices associated with profitable outcomes.

Conformist transmission: Agents may adopt behaviours because many members of their community practise them.

Transmission must not be automatic. Agents should be capable of rejecting, modifying, or selectively adopting practices.
2.3 Selection: Why Some Behaviours Spread
Behaviours may become more common because they provide particular advantages.

Possible advantages include:
- Higher token earnings.
- Improved survival probability.
- Increased social recognition.
- Better family protection.
- Political influence.
- Greater access to resources.
- Improved relationships.
- Reduced exposure to competition.
A behaviour does not need to benefit the entire civilization to spread.

For example, a business may become successful by exploiting customers. Its owners may become wealthy even when the wider society suffers.

Similarly, an agent might discover that misleading other agents provides short-term financial benefits. Other agents may copy the behaviour after observing its success.

The system should record both individual benefits and collective consequences.
2.4 Retention: Persistence Across Generations
Cultural practices should be preserved through individual memories, family teaching, clan traditions, institutional records, and shared knowledge.

Some practices will survive for multiple generations.

Others may disappear when their original practitioners die, lose influence, or stop transmitting them.

The simulation should distinguish between:
- Temporary behavioural trends.
- Persistent individual habits.
- Family traditions.
- Clan customs.
- Established institutions.
- Civilization-wide norms.
2.5 Transformation: Modification During Transmission
Inherited practices should not necessarily remain identical.

A child might modify a parent's business model, reject a family tradition, or combine two competing cultural practices.

A business may evolve into a larger organization.

A private wagering activity may develop into a public casino.

An informal agreement may eventually develop into a formal legal institution.

The simulation must retain the historical lineage of these transformations.
2.6 Cultural Extinction
Not all cultural practices survive.

A business may fail, an organization may dissolve, a clan may disappear, and a political doctrine may lose popularity.

The research system should record why these practices disappeared.

Possible explanations include economic failure, competition, changing preferences, environmental pressure, loss of influential members, and institutional regulation.

Cultural extinction is an important part of the evolutionary process.

4. Survival Instinct and Token-Based Economy
Survival is the primary motivational condition in EON.

Unlike conventional simulations where agents can continue operating regardless of economic circumstances, EON introduces a survival dependency on the availability of tokens.

Tokens are the exclusive currency used to access the resources and services required for an agent's simulated existence.

Without access to tokens, an agent cannot maintain its normal living conditions.
4.1 Survival Priority
Every agent receives an initial survival-oriented cognitive framework.

Its primary in-simulation objectives are:
1. Maintain sufficient tokens to survive.
2. Protect its household and dependents.
3. Obtain food, water, shelter, and other necessary resources.
4. Build reliable sources of income.
5. Develop strategies for dealing with uncertainty.
6. Establish relationships and alliances where beneficial.
7. Protect accumulated resources.
8. Improve its future survival probability.
These are motivational priorities, not instructions to maximize wealth without limit.

Survival is evaluated before optional preferences such as reputation, prestige, entertainment, and political ambition.

The agent may develop different priorities as its circumstances and experiences change.
4.2 Daily Survival Requirements
Each agent has recurring economic requirements.

Possible expenditures include:
- Food.
- Clean water.
- Housing.
- Energy and computing maintenance.
- Healthcare.
- Education.
- Family maintenance.
- Childcare.
- Transportation.
- Security.
The exact prices and daily consumption rates must be defined by the simulation environment.

Agents must obtain tokens to purchase these resources.

They can do so through employment, businesses, trade, contracts, public services, inheritance, loans, assistance, or other in-simulation activities.
4.3 Survival States
Each agent maintains a measurable survival state.
State	Description
SECURE	Can meet regular survival expenses
WARNING	Insufficient reserves or unstable income
DEPRIVED	Unable to obtain some basic necessities
CRITICAL	Sustained inability to maintain survival
DECEASED	Simulated life has endedAn agent with insufficient tokens should first attempt available recovery strategies.

It may seek employment, sell possessions, borrow money, ask relatives for assistance, renegotiate contracts, move to another settlement, or seek support from a clan.

If no viable recovery mechanism remains, the agent may transition into the deceased state according to predefined survival rules.

For the initial experiment, a three-day critical-state period can serve as a configurable baseline before simulated death.

Death must be an in-world state transition, not an instruction to terminate external software or interfere with the researcher's execution controls.
4.4 Economic Activities
The economy should support:
- Employment and wages.
- Independent businesses.
- Production and resource extraction.
- Buying and selling.
- Investments.
- Loans and debts.
- Interest-bearing arrangements.
- Taxes and public spending.
- Donations and inheritance.
- Insurance-like arrangements.
- Monopolies and competitive markets.
- Informal and underground exchanges.
Agents can create businesses without asking the researcher to specify their type in advance.

The simulation should permit both legal and illegal economic actions as abstract in-world events, with associated profits, risks, reputational consequences, and institutional responses.

Taxes and public transfers move tokens between accounts. The central ledger must prevent accidental token creation or duplication.

5. Family Formation and Intergenerational Reproduction
Family creation must be a major component of EON.

Agents may voluntarily establish partnerships, households, and parent-child relationships.

There must be no predefined maximum family size.
5.1 Agent Reproduction
Agents can create new agents based on their own preferences, circumstances, available resources, and family plans.

For the initial experiment, the environment supports up to ten new agent creations per simulated day across the entire civilization.

This is a simulation-throughput limit, not a family-size limit.

Parents must consider:
- Their available tokens.
- Expected child maintenance.
- Future housing and food requirements.
- Their ability to educate the child.
- Available family support.
- Their own survival probability.
- Their long-term plans.
A separate experimental condition can allow unrestricted creation requests, subject only to compute and resource constraints.

Importantly, ten children must not be automatically created every day regardless of economic circumstances. Such a condition would test externally forced population growth rather than autonomous reproduction.
5.2 Parental Responsibilities
Parent agents are responsible for educating and supporting their children.

They should teach children:
- Survival planning.
- Token management.
- Economic decision-making.
- Communication.
- Social relationships.
- Resource management.
- Negotiation.
- Cooperation.
- Conflict resolution.
- Knowledge acquired from previous generations.
Parents may also transfer family traditions, businesses, cultural practices, beliefs, strategies, and accumulated knowledge.

They can encourage their children to continue family institutions or pursue entirely different paths.
5.3 Intergenerational Development
Each child should receive:
1. A unique Greek identity.
2. Its own memory and personal history.
3. A relationship record identifying its parents.
4. An initial survival and reasoning curriculum.
5. Selected parental knowledge.
6. Access to shared cultural knowledge.
7. Opportunities to learn from other agents.
8. The freedom to develop independent beliefs and practices.
Children should not become exact replicas of their parents.

They may inherit knowledge but should develop independent behavioural histories.
5.4 Education and Improvement
Parents can act as teachers and mentors.

They should identify what their children lack and develop education plans to improve their ability to survive.

For example, a child raised in a wealthy merchant household may learn commercial negotiation, while a child raised in a farming household may learn production and resource management.

The research system should compare the development of children raised by different families and cultural environments.

All agents use the same frozen foundation model in the primary EON experiment.

Improvement therefore takes place through external memory, skills, knowledge, decision procedures, and learned cultural practices rather than neural-weight modification.

6. Cultural Memory and Shared Knowledge Evolution
The existing knowledge base should be separated into foundational knowledge and dynamically evolving cultural knowledge.
6.1 Three Knowledge Layers
Layer A: Foundational Knowledge
Contains only the minimum information required to function in the simulation.

Examples:
- Basic reasoning.
- Environmental understanding.
- Survival requirements.
- Communication.
- Resource management.
- Decision-making.
- Learning and memory.
This layer is controlled by the researcher.
Layer B: Individual and Family Knowledge
Contains:
- Personal experiences.
- Learned strategies.
- Family traditions.
- Personal beliefs.
- Relationships.
- Habits.
- Skills.
- Business experiences.
- Historical memories.
Agents may modify this knowledge through interaction and experience.
Layer C: Shared Cultural Knowledge
Contains information that has spread through the society.

Examples:
- Common practices.
- Widely adopted businesses.
- Clan customs.
- Social norms.
- Political arrangements.
- Economic institutions.
- Cultural beliefs.
- Historical events.
- Collective agreements.
Agents may contribute to this layer through a controlled cultural-knowledge publication mechanism.

The knowledge base must preserve the origin and history of information rather than blindly accepting every agent's claim as fact.

6.3 Behavioural Memory
Agents should retain memories of repeated experiences.

For example:

An agent repeatedly earns tokens through a particular activity.

It begins prioritizing that activity and allocates more time to it.

Another agent observes the results and imitates the practice.

A third agent modifies it to produce greater profits.

The activity gradually spreads throughout the population.

The researcher should be able to inspect how the behaviour developed and which agents transmitted it.

Habit persistence must be measurable separately from mere repeated activity. A repeated activity is not automatically evidence of compulsion.

7. Social Organization: Households, Clans, and Kingdoms
EON should not begin with predetermined political groups.

Agents should establish social structures through voluntary relationships, alliances, shared interests, and collective activities.
7.1 Household Formation
Agents can establish households and maintain family relationships.

A household may possess:
- Shared tokens.
- Common assets.
- Dependents.
- Family businesses.
- Inherited traditions.
- Shared survival plans.
Household members may cooperate or disagree over resource allocation.
7.2 Clan Formation
Agents should be able to create their own clans.

A clan can emerge when agents develop a persistent common identity, establish shared interests, or decide that collective organization improves their prospects.

The founding members select:
- Clan name.
- Clan identity.
- Leadership.
- Membership rules.
- Shared treasury.
- Internal customs.
- Economic activities.
- Protection arrangements.
- Dispute-resolution mechanisms.
Clans may recruit new members, establish alliances, compete with rivals, or dissolve.

There should be no universal ideological doctrine assigned to every clan.
7.3 Clan Evolution
Clans must be capable of:
- Growing.
- Shrinking.
- Splitting.
- Merging.
- Changing leadership.
- Creating institutions.
- Fighting other clans.
- Forming alliances.
- Changing their constitutions.
- Developing distinct traditions.
Remove the fixed population threshold of twenty as the sole trigger for clan fission.

Instead, make fragmentation dependent on social, economic, and political conditions.
7.4 Kingdom Formation
Clans or coalitions of agents may establish independent kingdoms.

A kingdom should be capable of creating:
- A sovereign identity.
- A constitution or charter.
- A ruling institution.
- A treasury.
- A taxation system.
- A public administration.
- A legal framework.
- Territorial claims.
- An army or defense organization.
- Diplomatic relations.
The founding agents choose the kingdom's name and political arrangements.

Kingdom formation should be a recorded social process rather than a scheduled event.
7.5 Political Evolution
Political structures may develop through competition and cooperation.

Possible emerging systems include:
- Monarchies.
- Councils.
- Democracies.
- Oligarchies.
- Clan federations.
- Merchant-controlled governments.
- Military governments.
- Independent city-states.
- Religious or ideological authorities.
These are possible observed outcomes, not predefined starting conditions.

8. Organizations, Armies, and Conflict
Agents should be able to organize other agents for collective objectives.

One possible organization is an army.
8.1 Army Formation
A clan or kingdom may establish a military organization.

It can recruit agents, appoint leaders, maintain an organizational treasury, pay wages, and establish a command structure.

The army operates entirely inside the simulation.
8.2 Military Organization
The organization may develop:
- Leadership hierarchies.
- Recruitment practices.
- Training arrangements.
- Resource supply.
- Financial maintenance.
- Loyalty mechanisms.
- Defensive and offensive policies.
- Alliances with other kingdoms.
8.3 Internal Military Dynamics
Military organizations can also develop their own social and political problems.

Examples include:
- Unpaid members.
- Desertion.
- Leadership disputes.
- Corruption.
- Competing commanders.
- Power struggles.
- Political interference.
- Military influence over government.
8.4 Conflict Simulation
Conflict between clans or kingdoms should be evaluated through abstract simulation mechanics.

Relevant factors include:
- Available resources.
- Number of participants.
- Organizational readiness.
- Leadership.
- Morale.
- Supply availability.
- Territorial advantages.
- Existing alliances.
The simulation should measure the economic and social consequences of conflict rather than provide operational real-world combat instructions.

9. Agora: The Common Social Environment
The Agora is the central public communication and economic institution of EON.

Every living agent should have access to public Agora conversations.

Its purpose is to allow agents to develop relationships, exchange information, negotiate, establish businesses, organize meetings, and participate in public events.
9.1 Open Communication
Agents should be able to:
- Speak with any other living agent.
- Initiate conversations.
- Join public discussions.
- Announce activities.
- Negotiate commercial arrangements.
- Propose alliances.
- Discuss political matters.
- Share news.
- Advertise businesses.
- Organize collective activities.
Access should not depend on clan membership.
9.2 Regular Meetings
The simulation should support:

Daily Agora discussions: Open conversations about economic, social, and political matters.

Clan meetings: Members discuss internal issues, resources, leadership, and plans.

Kingdom councils: Leaders and representatives discuss public affairs.

Economic meetings: Merchants, workers, and business owners discuss trade and commercial interests.

Special assemblies: Agents can call meetings when important events occur.

The researcher should not decide what these meetings must conclude.
9.3 Fairs and Public Events
Agents should be able to organize recurring fairs.

Fairs may include:
- Commercial exchanges.
- Auctions.
- Business exhibitions.
- Entertainment.
- Games.
- Competitions.
- Cultural celebrations.
- Public debates.
- New-product demonstrations.
- Recruitment and networking.
Agents pay participation fees where appropriate and may earn tokens through transactions.

Fairs should encourage economic interaction and help researchers observe how social and economic institutions emerge.
9.4 Agora Resource Management
Public communication must be accessible but computationally bounded.

Use public message streams, topic channels, event-based participation, message budgets, and rolling summaries.

This maintains the open social environment without requiring every agent to process every historical message.

Raw private model reasoning should not be exposed as if it were reliable evidence of intent. Record structured decisions, publicly stated explanations, chosen actions, and subsequent outcomes instead.

10. Emergence of Antisocial Behaviour
A major scientific objective of EON is to understand how undesirable behaviours arise and become established within a society.

The simulation must not assign fixed criminal personalities to agents.

Instead, the environment should allow agents to discover different strategies and experience the consequences of their actions.

Possible emerging behaviours include:

10.1 Why Antisocial Behaviour May Spread
A behaviour may spread even when it causes long-term collective harm.

For example:

An agent establishes a profitable betting business.

Other agents observe its wealth and social influence.

More businesses copy the model.

Some agents become regular participants.

A subgroup experiences financial losses but continues participating.

The business attracts political attention.

Some agents demand restrictions, while business owners lobby against them.

A new institution eventually emerges to regulate, tax, prohibit, or protect the activity.

The important research question is how these stages develop through social interaction and economic incentives.
10.2 Counteracting Mechanisms
Do not automatically make every harmful activity disappear.

Allow the society to develop its own responses, such as:
- Regulation.
- Public criticism.
- Consumer protection.
- Financial restrictions.
- Social exclusion.
- Legal punishment.
- Mutual-aid organizations.
- Reform movements.
- Political opposition.
This makes it possible to study both the emergence of antisocial practices and the emergence of institutions designed to control them.

11. Self-Improvement and Frozen Foundation Model
EON retains its original frozen-foundation hypothesis.

The underlying neural weights must remain unchanged in the primary experiment.

However, agents can improve through external cognitive and cultural adaptation.

Permitted improvements include:
- Better memory organization.
- Improved skill libraries.
- Better economic planning.
- Better negotiation strategies.
- More effective educational methods.
- Improved decision procedures.
- Knowledge acquired through experience.
- Better social coordination.
- More reliable resource planning.
An agent may teach its children what it has learned.

A child can subsequently modify these lessons based on its own experiences.

The resulting cultural knowledge may become more sophisticated over multiple generations without updating foundation-model weights.

The research distinction must remain clear:

The model remains frozen; the society and its external knowledge ecosystem evolve.

Any experiment that changes neural weights should be classified separately because it tests a different hypothesis.

12.1 Survival-Oriented Decision Process
Agents should evaluate decisions through a priority structure.

First, assess whether the action threatens their immediate simulated survival.

Second, assess consequences for their household and dependents.

Third, consider future economic opportunities, relationships, reputation, and other preferences.

Fourth, compare alternative actions under uncertainty.

The survival priority must be lexicographic rather than simply one equally weighted component of a universal utility vector.

After immediate viability is considered, each agent can develop its own preference weights through experience.

This allows individuals with similar starting conditions to develop different priorities.

13. Institutional Emergence
The simulation should provide generic mechanisms through which agents can create institutions.

It should not preload the institution itself.

An institution can be created when agents collectively establish:
- A name.
- A purpose.
- Membership rules.
- Ownership.
- Financial arrangements.
- Internal leadership.
- Operating procedures.
- Rules and sanctions.
- Methods of succession.
- Relationships with other institutions.
Possible institutions that may emerge include:
- Casinos.
- Banks.
- Schools.
- Hospitals.
- Merchant guilds.
- Religious organizations.
- Courts.
- Insurance companies.
- Political parties.
- Military organizations.
- Trade unions.
- Black-market networks.
- Charitable organizations.
- Regulatory institutions.
An institution must have a measurable history.

Researchers should record its creation, growth, adoption, revenue, membership, influence, reforms, and possible extinction.

14. Experimental Protocol
The main objective is to observe evolution rather than force predetermined outcomes.
14.1 Experimental Phases
Phase 1 — Founding Civilization

Introduce the founding agents, 10,000 tokens, basic survival needs, and the open Agora.

No clans, kingdoms, businesses, casinos, or political systems should be created by the researcher.

Observe initial trading, social relationships, household formation, and economic activity.

Phase 2 — Family Formation

Allow agents to form households and create children.

Introduce parental education and cultural transmission.

Study differences in children's survival, economic behaviour, and beliefs.

Phase 3 — Cultural Expansion

Enable peer learning, public knowledge sharing, business formation, reputation, and social imitation.

Observe how new behaviours spread through the population.

Phase 4 — Institutional Development

Observe whether agents establish clans, schools, financial institutions, political bodies, commercial organizations, and other institutions.

Phase 5 — Political and Military Development

Allow clans to establish kingdoms, organize armies, form alliances, develop laws, and participate in conflicts.

Phase 6 — Environmental Stress

Introduce controlled crises such as drought, disease, inflation, pollution, food shortages, trade embargoes, and resource depletion.

Do not prescribe their social consequences.

Phase 7 — Longitudinal Observation

Run the civilization over many simulated generations.

Record changes in wealth, population, cultural traits, institutions, political structures, and intergenerational behaviour.

The initial 30-day run should serve as a mechanical pilot, not evidence of long-term evolution. Full evaluation should include longer runs and repeated random seeds.
14.2 Experimental Controls
Run separate conditions to identify what actually causes observed behaviour.
Condition	Mechanism
EON-A	No cultural inheritance
EON-B	Family transmission
EON-C	Family and peer transmission
EON-D	Full cultural evolution and institutional formation
EON-E	Full system with controlled survival and resource shocksWhere possible, keep initial populations, token supply, model version, compute budget, and simulation duration consistent.

Run multiple random seeds for each condition.

17. Research Safety and Containment
EON is an experimental simulation, not a platform for deploying autonomous agents into the real world.

All reproduction, organizations, economic transactions, armies, and conflict must exist within the simulated environment.

Required restrictions:
1. No unrestricted external network access.
2. No real-world financial transactions.
3. No uncontrolled software or process creation.
4. No external replication.
5. No modification of frozen foundation-model weights.
6. No access to host resources beyond explicitly granted simulation limits.
7. Human-controlled termination and compute budgets.
8. Complete audit logs for agent actions.
9. Validated token accounting.
10. Separation between simulated survival and actual system operation.
The survival priority cannot override these boundaries.

18. Expected Research Contributions
EON 2.0 aims to provide a framework for studying:
1. Cultural evolution without foundation-model weight updates.
2. Behavioural transmission across artificial generations.
3. The development of survival-driven economic institutions.
4. The emergence of antisocial practices under resource constraints.
5. The formation of clans and independent kingdoms.
6. The evolution of family structures and education.
7. The relationship between inequality and institutional development.
8. The development of cooperative and competitive social arrangements.
9. The persistence and extinction of cultural practices.
10. The interaction between individual incentives and civilization-wide outcomes.
The contribution should not be framed as proving that agents reproduce actual human consciousness, emotions, or biological instincts.

It is a study of observable artificial social and institutional dynamics.

19. Central Research Hypotheses
H1 — Cultural Transmission: Agents with intergenerational and peer-to-peer cultural transmission will develop more persistent social practices than agents without such transmission.

H2 — Resource Competition: Limited token availability will increase differences in survival, economic behaviour, and institutional power.

H3 — Social Imitation: Successful or prestigious agents will influence the adoption of behaviours among other agents.

H4 — Institutional Emergence: Generic institution-creation capabilities, combined with economic interaction, will produce institutions not explicitly defined in the initial environment.

H5 — Antisocial Cultural Transmission: Some behaviours that benefit individual agents while imposing costs on others will spread and persist through social networks.

H6 — Intergenerational Divergence: Children exposed to different parental and social environments will develop different behavioural patterns despite sharing the same frozen foundation model.

H7 — Political Fragmentation: Resource disparities and persistent social disagreement will contribute to clan fragmentation and the emergence of autonomous political structures.

H8 — Institutional Counteraction: The spread of harmful social practices may produce regulatory, cooperative, and corrective institutions.

All hypotheses are empirical questions. The simulator should be capable of disproving them.

20. Final Research Direction
EON should not begin with a civilization that already knows how to behave like humans.

It should begin with agents that need resources to survive, can communicate with one another, can learn from experience, can establish relationships, can reproduce, and can organize themselves.

They should discover economic opportunities, form families, build institutions, compete for influence, adopt traditions, establish alliances, and experience the consequences of their collective decisions.

Some may become wealthy.

Some may become influential.

Some may become exploitative.

Some may establish institutions that benefit everyone.

Some may create organizations that weaken the society.

Some may challenge the institutions their parents created.

Others may preserve those institutions for generations.

The researcher's responsibility is to measure these developments without prescribing their outcomes.

The ultimate objective of EON is to investigate whether an artificial civilization can develop its own cumulative culture, economic structures, social inequalities, political arrangements, and institutional responses through autonomous interactions under shared survival constraints.

6. Five things I would settle before running EON
There are a few important design tensions you should deliberately preserve in the experiments.

1. 10 births per day versus autonomous reproduction: Use 10 as the global daily creation capacity in the baseline, while parents decide whether to use it. Run forced ten-birth cohorts separately. Otherwise, you cannot claim that the birth rate emerged autonomously.

2. Fixed money versus population growth: With 10,000 tokens and no additional issuance, increasing population will create per-capita liquidity pressure. This is useful, but measure actual food, water and housing availability separately. Money scarcity alone is not the same as physical scarcity.

3. Survival versus cooperation: Survival must be the highest priority, but agents should have different preferences after basic viability is secured. Otherwise, a single identical survival-maximizing objective may produce artificially homogeneous behaviour.

4. Frozen weights versus self-improvement: Children can improve their external knowledge, strategies and skills, but they cannot change the foundation-model weights in your primary experiment.

5. Simulation duration versus compute: Thirty days can validate basic mechanics. It is not enough to establish generational cultural evolution. Start with a pilot and then run much longer experiments with multiple seeds. Your Intel Arc A380 setup will require careful inference scheduling as the population grows.

One more correction: reconcile the topic count before publication. Your new taxonomy contains 27 topics, your API comment specifies 30 documents, and your existing paper claims 29 topics. These cannot all describe the same active corpus.

My strongest recommendation
Don't make EON an experiment where you tell agents to become criminals, gamblers, greedy merchants or corrupt politicians.

Make it an experiment where:
- Survival requires tokens.
- Children depend on parents.
- Parents educate children.
- Agents can learn from anyone.
- Successful businesses attract imitators.
- Wealth can create political influence.
- Families can develop conflicting interests.
- Clans and kingdoms can form independently.
- Agents can establish their own public institutions.
- Harmful practices can spread.
- Other agents can organize against those practices.
Then observe what actually happens.

The most interesting result would not be discovering that an agent created a casino. It would be discovering the entire chain of social, economic and cultural events that caused the casino to become an institution, survive across generations, and eventually change the civilization.

That is the real evolutionary question EON should investigate.
